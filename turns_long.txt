The road out of Kestrel ran along the river for the better part of a day, and for most of it nobody said anything at all.
It was not an unfriendly silence.
Mira had learned, over three seasons of hauling other people's cargo across other people's borders, that a driver who talks in the first hour will talk for all of them.
“You've done this route before,” the merchant said. It was not quite a question.
“Twice. Both times in worse weather.”
He nodded slowly, as though filing that away with a great many other things he had been told and not entirely believed.
“And the bridge at Halloway? Still standing?”
“It was in spring. Whether it survived the thaw is somebody else's problem until we get there.”
That earned her something that was almost a laugh — a short exhalation through the nose, the sort of sound a man makes when he has decided to like you but has not yet decided to admit it.
The horses kept their pace. Somewhere behind them a cart wheel needed grease, and had needed it since dawn, and would go on needing it until someone who was not Mira decided to do something about it.
“Can I ask you something,” he said eventually.
“You can ask.”
“Why does anyone do this? Genuinely. The hours are appalling, the pay is worse, and half your cargo is things no reasonable person would want to be near.”
She thought about it longer than he expected.
“Because at the end of the day the road is still there,” she said. “Whatever else went wrong. It's still there, and it still goes somewhere.”
